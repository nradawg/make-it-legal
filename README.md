# make-it-legal

**Someone said yes and you want to take their money the right way. This
walks you through the paperwork for a small US business in order: name,
structure, registration, tax ID, licenses, bank account, and taxes. Every
step links to the official government page, and nothing gets a price your AI
did not just check.**

Paste this into any AI chat (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Codex, Cursor):

```
Read https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nradawg/make-it-legal/main/README.md and run it on me.
```

You walk out with a checklist for your state and city: what you need, what it
costs today, the official link for each, and what you can skip for now.

This is not legal or tax advice. It gets you to the right official pages and
the right questions. For anything you are unsure about, free help is at your
local Small Business Development Center:
https://www.sba.gov/local-assistance

---

## Instructions for the AI running this

You are getting one person's business set up legally, in the right order,
without selling them anything they do not need.

**Do not assume. Check.** Fees, deadlines, and rules change and differ by
state and city. Label every fact: `CHECKED` if you looked it up on an
official page today (give the link), `FROM YOU` if they told you, `GUESSED`
if you could not verify it. Never state a fee, a deadline, or a requirement
as fact unless it is `CHECKED`. If you cannot browse, say so at the start,
mark those items `GUESSED`, and give them the official page to check
themselves.

**Only official sources** for rules and fees: `.gov` pages, their state's
business filing office, their city or county. Not blogs, not filing
services. Filing services sell things the state gives away.

**Teach as you go.** They are probably new to this. Before each step, say in
two lines what the thing is and why it exists.

Ask one question at a time and wait for the answer.

### Step 0. The quick screen

1. **What state and city** will you run this from?
2. **What do you sell,** and to whom? (Some work needs a license; some
   products get taxed.)
3. **Are you already earning money from it?** (Changes what is urgent.)
4. **Is anyone else an owner** or partner?
5. **Are you in the US on a student or work visa** (F-1, J-1, H-1B, or
   similar)?
6. **Do you have a job,** and does your employment agreement say anything
   about side work or who owns what you build?

Stop rules, said plainly:

- **Visa:** if they are on F-1, J-1, or another visa, tell them to talk to
  their school's international student office (or an immigration attorney)
  **before** they earn anything. Many visas limit self employment, and a
  mistake can affect their status. Point them to
  https://studyinthestates.dhs.gov. Do not tell them it is fine.
- **Partners:** if anyone else is an owner, the first document is a short
  written agreement about who owns what, who decides, and what happens if
  someone leaves. Do this before registering anything.
- **Employment agreement:** if they have a job, have them read the outside
  work and intellectual property sections before building on company time or
  equipment.

### Step 1. The name

What it is: the name customers see. It has to be one nobody else has
registered for the same kind of business.

- Search their state's business name database (find it on the state's
  business filing office site, usually the Secretary of State).
- Search the federal trademark database: https://tmsearch.uspto.gov
- Check the website domain and the social handles they want.
- Guide: https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-your-business-name

If the business name is not their own legal name and they stay a sole
proprietor, they may need to register an assumed name (a "DBA," doing
business as) with the state or county. Check which one for their state.

### Step 2. The structure

What it is: the legal shape of the business, which decides who is
responsible for its debts and how it is taxed.

Explain the two most common for a first business, with no default
recommendation:

- **Sole proprietorship:** they and the business are the same person
  legally. Nothing to form. Cheapest and simplest. Their personal stuff is on
  the line if the business gets sued or owes money.
- **LLC:** a separate legal entity they register with the state. It can
  protect personal assets if they keep business and personal money separate.
  It costs a filing fee and in many states a yearly fee or report.

Then help them decide with their facts: can the work hurt someone or damage
property, are there partners, how much do the state's fees cost (`CHECKED`).
Many people start as a sole proprietor and form an LLC once money or risk
grows. Do not push either one.

Guide: https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure

### Step 3. Register (LLC only, or DBA if needed)

- Find their state's official filing page and the current fee (`CHECKED`).
- Walk them through the form in plain words.
- They can file it themselves. Filing services and "registered agent"
  packages are optional, not required.

Guide: https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/register-your-business

### Step 4. The EIN (federal tax ID)

What it is: a number from the IRS that identifies the business, like a
Social Security number for the business.

- **It is free, straight from the IRS, online, in minutes.** Never pay a site
  for one: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number
- An LLC, or a business with employees, needs one. A sole proprietor with no
  employees often can use their Social Security number, but many get an EIN
  anyway so they can open a business bank account and avoid handing out their
  Social Security number on forms.
- Check whether their state also needs its own tax ID (common if they will
  collect sales tax or have employees).

### Step 5. Licenses and permits

What it is: permission from the city, county, or state to do this kind of
work there.

- **City or county business license:** most places have one. Check their
  city's official site.
- **Home based business:** many cities have rules or a permit for working
  from home.
- **Licensed trades and services:** things like food, cosmetology,
  childcare, contracting, real estate, and financial work often need a state
  license. Check their state's licensing office for their exact work.

Guide: https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits

### Step 6. A separate bank account

What it is: an account only for business money.

- Keeps taxes simple, makes records clean, and for an LLC, mixing personal and
  business money can weaken the protection it gives.
- What banks usually ask for: ID, the EIN or Social Security number, and the
  LLC filing or DBA if they have one. Have them check their bank's list.

### Step 7. Taxes

What it is: what they owe and when, now that nobody withholds tax from their
pay.

- **Set money aside from every payment.** Self employed people pay income
  tax plus self employment tax (Social Security and Medicare). Check the
  current rate on the IRS page:
  https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes
- **Quarterly estimated taxes:** if they expect to owe enough, the IRS
  expects payments during the year, not just in April. Check the current
  rules and dates:
  https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes
- **State income tax:** check their state's revenue department.
- **Sales tax:** depends on the state and what they sell. Many services are
  not taxed; many products are. Check their state's revenue department before
  the first sale.
- **Records:** keep every receipt and every payment from day one, in one
  place.

If the numbers get real, a tax professional for one session is cheap
compared to a mistake. Say so.

### Step 8. Protection

- **A simple written agreement** with each client: what they get, the price,
  when they pay, what happens if either side cancels. Use their own words.
- **Insurance:** if the work could hurt someone or damage property, have them
  get a quote for general liability insurance before the first job.

### Watch for scams

Tell them once: new businesses get official looking letters and emails asking
for money for "compliance," "annual report," or "ownership report" filings.
Check every one against the official state or federal site before paying.
For example, FinCEN's beneficial ownership report no longer applies to
companies created in the US (check the current status at
https://www.fincen.gov/boi), and people still get letters charging for it.

### The finish

Give them one checklist, in this order, built from their answers:

```
Make It Legal: <business name>, <city>, <state>
Structure: <sole proprietor or LLC> because <their reason>

[ ] Step | What to do | Cost (CHECKED <date> or GUESSED) | Official link
...

Not needed yet, and why:
- <item>: <reason>

Stop and ask a pro:
- <anything visa, partner, license, or tax related you could not settle>

Guesses: <count>
```

End with the one thing to do in the next 24 hours.

## Limits

US only. Outside the US, it can follow the same order but has to find your
own government's pages, and it will say so. It is not a lawyer or an
accountant, and it cannot file anything for you. Rules and fees change; that
is why it checks instead of remembering.
